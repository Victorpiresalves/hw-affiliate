How the test is done

Sit down and find the flat, bony front of the shin just above the ankle bone, or the top of the foot. Press one thumb straight down into the skin, firmly but not hard enough to hurt, and hold it there for about five seconds. Then lift your thumb and look at the spot.

Two things can happen. The skin fills back in immediately, and within a second or two you cannot tell where you pressed. Or a small crater is left behind — a dent with a visible edge that takes several seconds, sometimes much longer, to fill back in. The second result is what is meant by pitting, and the whole point of the test is that it separates “this leg looks puffy” from “there is loose fluid sitting in this tissue.”

Do it on the same spot on both legs, and do it at roughly the same time of day. Fluid follows gravity, so a leg tested at ten in the morning and a leg tested at eight at night are not being compared fairly.

What the dent actually means

Your smallest blood vessels are slightly leaky by design. All day, a small amount of watery fluid pushes out of the capillaries into the space between your cells, where it bathes the tissue. That is normal and it is supposed to happen. What matters is the return trip: most of that fluid is reabsorbed, and the remainder is collected by the lymphatic system and carried back toward the neck, where it rejoins the bloodstream.

When more fluid arrives in the tissue than is carried away, the leftover collects in that in-between space. Skin with extra fluid in it behaves differently under a thumb. Instead of springing back, the fluid is squeezed sideways and then takes time to seep back into the hollow you made. The dent is not the problem itself; the dent is what a lag in the return trip looks like from the outside.

That is also why a sock line at noon is a slower version of the same test. An elastic band is a thumb that presses for hours instead of five seconds.

How the dent is graded

Clinicians describe pitting on a rough scale, and it is worth knowing that different clinics word it differently — some grade by how deep the dent is, some by how long it takes to disappear, most by a bit of both. Read the version below as a way to describe what you saw, not as a diagnosis you can hand yourself.

  • Barely there. A shallow mark, a millimetre or two, gone in about a second or two. A salty meal or a hot day can do this.
  • Clearly visible. A dent you can point at, perhaps four millimetres deep, taking on the order of five seconds to fill back in.
  • Deep and slow. A crater you could rest a fingertip in, still visible after eight seconds and sometimes after a minute or more, with the skin over it looking stretched or shiny.

Swelling that appears during or right after a long flight or a long drive, especially in one leg, is not on this scale at all: see the box below.

A dent that is deep and slow, that is new, or that is getting worse week over week is a reason to book an appointment — not a reason to buy anything. So is swelling that does not settle overnight after your legs have been horizontal for hours.

One distinction surprises most people: not all swelling pits. Some kinds of tissue swelling are firm and leave no mark at all under a thumb, and telling those apart is one of the things a clinician is looking for when they run the same test you just ran. That is a reason to have a professional look, not a reason to keep testing yourself.

Where the fluid is supposed to go

If the return trip is the part that lags, it is worth knowing how that return trip works. Blood has a central pump: the heart, beating whether you think about it or not. Lymph has nothing of the kind. Lymphatic vessels have their own small muscular walls that contract in a slow rhythm, and beyond that the system relies on ordinary movement — walking, the flex of a calf muscle, the change in pressure inside your chest each time you breathe deeply — to keep fluid moving along.

That is the whole reason walking, elevation and hydration are the standard advice you have already been given. A leg that spends nine hours under a desk, motionless, has almost nothing pushing on it. It is also why the mark tends to be worst at the end of the day and better after a night flat in bed: the system got a few hours of help from gravity.

None of that makes a supplement a fix for anything. It is context, and it is the reason a sensible daily routine starts with movement.
