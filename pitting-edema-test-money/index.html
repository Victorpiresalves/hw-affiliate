You read that you could press a thumb into the bone at the front of your ankle, hold it for five seconds and let go. So you did it, a minute ago, sitting where you are sitting now. The skin did not spring back. There is a small crater with a visible edge, and you are still looking at it.

That crater has a name. Clinicians call it pitting, and the reason they bother to press on ankles at all is that it separates two different sentences. “This leg looks puffy” is one sentence. “There is loose fluid sitting in this tissue” is another. The dent is the second one.

What the dent does not tell you is which cause put the fluid there. That is decided by two other things — how deep the dent is, and how long it takes to fill back in — plus a short list of red flags that change the answer completely. Doing the test properly, so the answer means something

Sit down and find the flat, bony front of the shin just above the ankle bone, or the top of the foot. Press one thumb straight down into the skin, firmly but not hard enough to hurt, and hold it there for about five seconds. Then lift your thumb and look at the spot.

Two things can happen. The skin fills back in immediately, and within a second or two you cannot tell where you pressed. Or a small crater is left behind — a dent with a visible edge that takes several seconds, sometimes much longer, to fill back in. The second result is the one that brought you here.

Three things make the result honest. Press the same spot on both legs. Do it at roughly the same time of day, because fluid follows gravity and a leg tested at ten in the morning is not the same leg at eight at night. And count the seconds out loud — almost everybody underestimates how long the dent stayed, and the number of seconds is half of the description.

What the dent actually means

Your smallest blood vessels are slightly leaky by design. All day, a small amount of watery fluid pushes out of the capillaries into the space between your cells, where it bathes the tissue. That is normal and it is supposed to happen. What matters is the return trip: most of that fluid is reabsorbed, and the remainder is collected by the lymphatic system and carried back toward the neck, where it rejoins the bloodstream.

When more fluid arrives in the tissue than is carried away, the leftover collects in that in-between space. Skin with extra fluid in it behaves differently under a thumb. Instead of springing back, the fluid is squeezed sideways and then takes time to seep back into the hollow you made. The dent is not the problem itself; the dent is what a lag in the return trip looks like from the outside.

Which is also why a sock line at noon is a slower version of the same test. An elastic band is a thumb that presses for hours instead of five seconds. If you have been noticing the trenches your socks leave by the end of the day, you have been running this test for months without calling it one.

The three ways the dent is described

Clinicians describe pitting on a rough scale, and it is worth knowing that different clinics word it differently — some grade by how deep the dent is, some by how long it takes to disappear, most by a bit of both. Read the version below as a way to describe what you saw, not as a diagnosis you can hand yourself.

Barely there. A shallow mark, a millimetre or two, gone in about a second or two. A salty meal or a hot day can do this.
Clearly visible. A dent you can point at, perhaps four millimetres deep, taking on the order of five seconds to fill back in.
Deep and slow. A crater you could rest a fingertip in, still visible after eight seconds and sometimes after a minute or more, with the skin over it looking stretched or shiny.

Swelling that appears during or right after a long flight or a long drive, especially in one leg, is not on this scale at all: it belongs in the red box above.

A dent that is deep and slow, that is new, or that is getting worse week over week is a reason to book an appointment — not a reason to buy anything. So is swelling that does not settle overnight after your legs have been horizontal for hours.

One distinction surprises most people: not all swelling pits. Some kinds of tissue swelling are firm and leave no mark at all under a thumb, and telling those apart is one of the things a clinician is looking for when they run the same test you just ran. That is a reason to have a professional look, not a reason to keep testing yourself.

What you have already tried, and why the dent came back

Almost nobody arrives at a page like this on day one. By the time you are pressing your own ankle to see what happens, you have usually tried two or three of these. Here is the honest version of each — including the two you should not stop.

Compression socks

They work while they are on. An elastic garment presses the fluid out of the tissue and holds it out, and that is a real, physical, immediate thing. Then you take them off at night, gravity resumes in the morning, and the ankle is where it was. That is not a failure of the sock; that is what a garment is. If a doctor put you in them, keep wearing them. Nothing on this page is a reason to stop.

Water pills

A diuretic is a prescription medicine that acts on the kidneys to move water out through the blood. It is a serious medication with real effects on your salts and your kidneys, and only the person who prescribed it should change it. Never stop or reduce a prescribed medication because of an advertisement, including this one. If you take one, that is a conversation with your doctor or pharmacist before you add anything at all.

Putting your legs up

This one genuinely helps, and it helps for exactly as long as your legs are up. Gravity spends the night on your side, the mark is smaller in the morning, and then the day starts again and the fluid has nine hours to come back. It is a good habit. It is not a schedule anyone can live inside.

Nothing yet

The most common answer, and a reasonable one, because the swelling arrived slowly enough that there was never a day when it obviously started. That is also why people are surprised by their own thumb: nothing changed today, but the dent has been filling in a little slower each year.

Where the fluid is supposed to go

If the return trip is the part that lags, it is worth knowing how that return trip works. Blood has a central pump: the heart, beating whether you think about it or not. Lymph has nothing of the kind. Lymphatic vessels have their own small muscular walls that contract in a slow rhythm, and beyond that the system relies on ordinary movement — walking, the flex of a calf muscle, the change in pressure inside your chest each time you breathe deeply — to keep fluid moving along.

That is the whole reason walking, elevation and hydration are the standard advice you have already been given. A leg that spends nine hours under a desk, motionless, has almost nothing pushing on it. It is also why the mark tends to be worst at the end of the day and better after a night flat in bed: the system got a few hours of help from gravity.

None of that makes a supplement a fix for anything. It is context, and it is the reason a sensible daily routine starts with movement.
