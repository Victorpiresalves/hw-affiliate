Cleavers — Galium aparine, aerial parts

Cleavers is the sprawling spring weed of the bedstraw family, the one with whorls of narrow leaves and tiny hooked bristles that make it cling to socks and dog fur. The part used is the aerial parts: the stem and leaf of the whole above-ground plant, gathered in spring before the plant flowers and turns coarse.

In Western herbalism it carries the nickname “lymphatic broom.” That is a traditional name and a piece of folk vocabulary — it is not a description of what the plant does in the body, and it should not be read as one.

One detail of the botany, which this page cannot resolve for any particular product: traditional practice strongly prefers cleavers fresh. The old texts are unusually consistent about this, and fresh-plant tinctures of cleavers are common for exactly that reason. It is a plant that turns up in liquid formulas more often than in dried powder capsules — a fact about herbal practice, not a claim about outcomes.

Red clover — Trifolium pratense, blossom

The familiar pink-purple clover of pastures and roadsides. The part used here is the blossom: the flower head, not the leaf and not the whole plant. If a label says only “red clover” without naming the part, it is telling you less than it should.

It has a long history in Western herbal practice and appears in a great many traditional combination formulas. It is also the ingredient here that people most often ask a pharmacist about — a good question to ask. If you are pregnant or nursing, if you take a blood thinner, or if you are under a doctor’s care for a hormone-sensitive condition, ask your own clinician or pharmacist before taking red clover in any form. We are not going to tell you it is fine for you — that is a conversation for someone who knows your history.

Stillingia — Stillingia sylvatica, root

The one most people have never heard of. Stillingia is a perennial native to the sandy soils of the American Southeast, known in the older texts as queen’s root or queen’s delight. The part used is the root.

It is a classic ingredient of nineteenth-century American herbal formulas, where it almost never appears on its own: it is a combination-formula botanical, used in small proportion alongside other plants. The old literature also notes that the dried root loses its character with age, which is why traditional preparations favored root that had been recently harvested.

Because it is unfamiliar, confirm the botanical name and the part used on the label rather than assuming — and note that a proprietary blend will not tell you how much of it is in the bottle.

Prickly ash — Zanthoxylum, bark

A thorny North American shrub or small tree. The part used is the bark. Two species share the common name — Zanthoxylum americanum in the north and Zanthoxylum clava-herculis in the south — which is a good illustration of why a common name on its own is not enough to compare two products.

Its old country name is the “toothache tree,” and that is worth explaining rather than hiding: the name comes from the tingling, numbing sensation people report when they chew the fresh bark. It is a description of how the bark tastes on the tongue. It is not a statement about what a supplement does, and nothing on this page should be read that way.

Nineteenth-century American herbals used the bark almost exclusively inside combination formulas rather than on its own.

Liquid extract or capsule: what actually differs

A capsule holds ground dried plant material. What you get is milligrams of powdered herb. A liquid extract holds the plant’s soluble constituents pulled into a solvent — usually water and alcohol, sometimes glycerin. What you get is expressed as a strength or a ratio per millilitre, not as milligrams of powder. They are two different things in two different containers, and comparing the numbers on their labels directly does not work.

Neither format is automatically better, and a page that tells you one of them is should be read with suspicion. What genuinely differs, in practical terms:

  • Solvent. Most Western tinctures use alcohol to pull the plant constituents into solution; some use glycerin or water instead. The seller of the product advertised here states that its formula contains no alcohol, and the full panel is on its label.
  • Dose flexibility. A dropper lets you take half a dose, or work up gradually. A capsule is one fixed unit, and splitting it is not practical.

One more note, so as not to oversell the format: the seller instructs that the drops be held under the tongue. Whether taking these particular plant constituents that way changes how the body handles them is not something this page can tell you, and we will not pretend otherwise.

What to read on any label — including this one

This checklist applies to the product advertised here exactly as much as to anything you are weighing it against.

  1. The botanical name, not just the common name. “Prickly ash” can be two species. “Clover” can be several plants.
  2. The part used. Root, bark, blossom and aerial parts are genuinely different ingredients from the same plant.
  3. The amount per serving, and what a serving is. If the serving is 1 mL and you intend to take 2 mL twice a day, the label is describing a quarter of your day.
  4. The strength or extract ratio, if the product is an extract rather than a powder.
  5. The solvent — water, alcohol, glycerin — and the alcohol content, if there is any.
  6. Whether it is a proprietary blend. A blend gives you one combined total. It does not tell you how the four plants are divided inside it, and no amount of reading will get that number off the label.
  7. Servings per container. This is how you compare cost per day instead of cost per bottle, which is the comparison that actually matters.
  8. Manufacturing and testing statements, and where the seller’s refund terms live.
